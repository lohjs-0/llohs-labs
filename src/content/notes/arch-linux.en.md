at some point, while looking at my perfectly functional computer, i thought: what if i made things a little more complicated?

that's pretty much how my interest in arch linux started.

not because i needed another operating system. windows was right there, working perfectly fine, minding its own business. but apparently that wasn't enough.

arch has always interested me because of the freedom to configure almost everything. you choose the components, build your environment, and decide how you want the system to work.

in other words, the freedom to do whatever you want. including making terrible decisions.

## it started with a relatively innocent idea

i wanted to explore linux more deeply.

understand how the system works, mess around with configuration files, learn about package management, and figure out what actually happens behind a graphical interface.

then i discovered hyprland.

and suddenly, i also wanted a minimalist desktop, lots of keyboard shortcuts, neatly organized windows, and a terminal i'd probably open fifty times a day.

because apparently learning linux wasn't enough. i also needed my computer to look like something from a hacker movie.

## documentation became part of the experience

one of the things i find most interesting about arch is how many decisions are left to the user.

bootloaders, partitioning, services, permissions, packages, desktop environments...

things you normally don't think about when all you want to do is turn on your computer and use it.

and there's something fascinating about realizing that an operating system isn't one mysterious thing. it's made up of several components that need to work together.

the problem is that when one of those components decides not to cooperate, the investigation begins.

my thought process usually goes something like this:

> i think i understand.

> wait, no.

> why did that happen?

> *opens the arch wiki.*

and there goes another forty minutes reading about something i didn't even know existed.

## the terminal isn't magic

one thing i've been trying to change about how i study technology is the habit of copying commands and hoping they work.

because running a command is easy.

understanding exactly what it does, which files it modifies, which permissions it requires, and what could happen if something goes wrong is a different story.

and arch seems like a great environment for developing that habit.

there's not much point in having a beautiful terminal with transparency and an elaborate configuration if i have no idea what's happening behind it.

although, to be fair, a pretty terminal definitely helps with motivation.

## the goal was never just a pretty desktop

as much as i like the idea of customizing every little detail, my interest goes beyond appearances.

i want to better understand how the system boots, how processes are managed, how services work, and how different parts of linux connect.

i also find it interesting how a simple attempt to customize a desktop can lead to topics like system architecture, memory management, permissions, and security.

you start by looking up how to change the top bar, and before you know it, you're reading about how systemd works.

a perfectly normal sequence of events, i'd say.

## i still have plenty of things left to break

i'm not going to pretend i've mastered arch or that i understand everything about linux.

there's still a lot to explore, concepts to study, and probably some very questionable mistakes waiting to happen.

i also don't know whether i'll ever use arch as my main operating system.

maybe.

or maybe i'll discover that i enjoy configuring operating systems more than actually using them.

which, now that i think about it, would explain a lot.

but that freedom to experiment is exactly what interests me. i don't just want to use an operating system; i want to understand why it works, how its components interact, and what happens when something stops working.

in the end, all this curiosity will probably result in a heavily customized desktop, several configuration files, hours of research, and a system i'll use to open a browser, listen to music, and write code.

exactly what i was doing before.

**the difference is that now i can break everything myself.**
